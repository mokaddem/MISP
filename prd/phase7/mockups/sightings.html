Sightings — four candidates

MISP computes a decay curve for every attribute and has never had anywhere to draw it against the sightings that move it. All four candidates put the two on one axis; what they disagree about is what the rest of the tab is for — a table under the chart, a drawer beside it, a lane per organisation, or a chronology the curve runs down.

S1 One overlay and one brush: the curve is drawn through the bars that move it, and the table underneath shows exactly the stretch you dragged.
Optimises for

Gives up

What MISP cannot supply today

All four candidates draw the same overlay, and all four lean on the same gap. These are findings for §7.7, not problems to design around quietly.

  1. The decay curve is per attribute, not per value. DecayingModel::getScoreOvertime($user, $model_id, $attribute_id, $overrides) takes one attribute id, and its base score is computed from that attribute’s type and its numerically-tagged taxonomies — so ten occurrences of one value can carry ten different curves for one model. There is no value-scoped decay endpoint and no rule in MISP for what “the value’s score” is. Every candidate here draws one line per model and therefore assumes one: the recommendation below is the maximum across occurrences, labelled with the occurrence it came from.
  2. The curve’s axis is not the histogram’s axis. getScoreOvertime returns hourly samples from the attribute’s own first timestamp to last sighting + lifetime — it starts wherever the attribute did and runs into the future. Putting it over a 90-day sighting window means resampling it in PHP, and deciding whether to draw the part of the curve that has not happened yet.
  3. False positives and expirations move nothing. getScoreOvertime asks listSightings(…, $sightingsType = 0), so only a plain sighting resets the decay clock. That is a fact worth putting on the chart, and each candidate does.
  4. Organisation attribution is optional. Plugin.Sightings_anonymise blanks the name and org_id of every sighting not from your organisation; generateStatistics then files them under Others. An org-stacked histogram becomes two colours, and S3 becomes two lanes.
  5. The count is per viewer. Plugin.Sightings_policy can hide whole sightings (event owner / sighting reporter / host org). 47 is what this user can see, not what the instance holds — which is why every candidate carries the ACL footer.
  6. Range is capped. MISP.Sightings_range (365 days by default) bounds the statistics series, so an “All time” option cannot be served from attributesStatistics() and needs its own query.
  7. No sighting carries a score contribution. S4’s per-row NIDS 55 → 80 is derived by re-running the model formula at each row; nothing in the API returns it.
  8. Cost. getScoreOvertime loops hourly from the attribute’s first timestamp to the end of its lifetime, per attribute, per model. Ten occurrences × two models is twenty such loops per page load; this panel needs a coarser server-side sampler or a cache before it goes near a busy instance.
  9. The write is real, and it fans out. Sighting::saveSightings(false, [$value], $timestamp, $user, $type, $source) matches on Attribute.value1 / value2 and writes one row per matching attribute the user can see. So the value-scoped “Add sighting” the brief asks for already exists in the model — but one click becomes ten rows, and every candidate says so on the control rather than in a confirmation dialog nobody reads.

Recommendation

S1, with two grafts.

S1 is the only one of the four that puts the whole argument on one screen without asking for an interaction first: the curve through the bars, the range under it, the rows under that, and the models beside it in the rail card the page already owns. It is also the one that costs least — two ajax panels in the shape phases 3 to 5 established — and the one whose failure mode is mild, since a narrow chart is still a chart.

Graft one: S4’s gap rows into S1’s table. A row reading 8 days with no sighting · NIDS 80 → 55 · crossed below 60 on 2025-08-07, inserted between day groups, buys the single best idea in the set — that the silence between sightings is the thing the curve is made of — for the price of a loop in one element, and without S4’s vertical rail or its oldest-first ordering.

Graft two: S3’s baseline split into S1’s histogram. Drawing false positives and expirations below the baseline rather than as another segment on top of the stack costs nothing, and it makes the one thing the chart must never blur — that a false positive contradicts the value rather than adding to it — impossible to misread.

If the pick is instead about ambition rather than cost, take S4 whole: it is the only candidate whose layout is an argument, and on the sparse values that are the majority case it is the one that reads best. It is an L, and its rail is the one piece here that a later CSS change could quietly break.
