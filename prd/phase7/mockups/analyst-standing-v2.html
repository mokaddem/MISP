Three ways to show the split

The Where the organisations stand card has one job: make disagreement between organisations visible before anyone reads an average. The prototype does say that, but it says it three times over in three encodings, and two of them contradict each other. Here are three replacements — one that fixes the scale, one that abandons it, and one that fuses the chart into the table.

Diagnosis

What the current card gets wrong

Five of these are defects rather than preferences — they produce a card that states two different things about the same number, or hides the answer it was built to give. Switch the fixture value above and each one is reproducible.

  • defect

    The histogram is coloured backwards from the table

    $side = $b < 5 ? 'ben' : 'mal' paints 0–50 green and 50–100 red. The table paints scores above 50 green (supports the value) and below 50 red. On 8.8.8.8 the two green bars at 0–20 are precisely the two organisations the table shows in red — one card, two opposite encodings of one number.

  • defect

    Organisation labels collide even when the markers do not

    The merge threshold guards the 34-unit discs and ignores the text above them. On 8.8.8.8 (scores 8 and 15) the discs clear it, so CIRCL and CthulhuSPRL.be overlap. On a card titled Where the organisations stand, names running into each other is the one failure it cannot have.

  • defect

    Merging discs deletes the names

    On 104.21.34.198 two organisations become a ×2 disc labelled 2 organisations · 10–12. The overlap is resolved by removing the card's primary content; the names survive only in a <title> tooltip and in the table below.

  • defect

    A red “Neutral”, and a green one

    MISP's five band words split the axis at 20/40/60/80; the reading splits it at 50. So Neutral · 45/100 takes a red badge and Neutral · 60/100 a green one. Two rows labelled Neutral in opposite colours, and nothing on the card explains why.

  • defect

    Staleness is invisible

    On 45.155.205.233, CERT-EU's opinion is 102 days old and CIRCL's is one day old. Both render as identical plain monospace dates. For an analyst weighing two conflicting opinions, which one is current is not a detail.

  • layout

    Three encodings of four numbers

    Each organisation's score is drawn as a strip marker, as a histogram bar, and twice inside its own table row (a badge and a .vp-opinion bar). The histogram resolves ten bands over three or four data points, so its count row renders as six middots and three ones, in a quarter of the card's width.

  • layout

    The strip stacks seven bands of content with no gestalt

    Name, date, disc, leader line, band boxes, numeric axis, mean caption. The two things a reader wants — who, and where — sit at opposite ends of that stack, and the mean's caption is ninety units from the marker it describes.

  • layout

    Band labels are drawn as boxes inside the track

    Five filled rectangles in the track's own grey, separated by body-background hairlines: a continuous 0–100 axis rendered as a five-segment control, competing with the 0/25/50/75/100 ticks directly beneath it.

Proposal 01

Aligned rail

Keep the 0–100 scale — it is the axis the Verdict tab already taught the reader — and fix its execution. One continuous rail, the empty middle cut into the rail as a hatched void rather than annotated above it, and organisation labels that dodge upward into as many rows as they need so no two ever touch and no marker is ever merged away.

Aligned rail

fix the axis

The rail carries position; the label carries identity; the table carries everything that is not a position. Nothing is drawn twice.

What it fixes
  • Labels dodge into stacked rows, so names never collide and markers never merge.
  • The gap is a void cut into the rail — the emptiness is in the object, not a floating bracket.
  • The mean hangs off its own position on a stem, so number and place are one element.
  • The histogram is gone; each score is drawn once on the rail and once as a numeral.
What it costs
  • Label dodging needs a measure-then-place pass in JS — the strip stops being pure static SVG.
  • Card height varies with how many label rows the data forces.
  • Above ~12 organisations the label stack grows taller than the table beneath it.
Proposal 02

Two camps

Stop asking the reader to decode an axis. The question is who is on which side, so answer it with layout: two facing columns, and between them a gutter that physically contains the empty middle. When the mean falls in that gutter, the card's whole argument is a fact about the layout rather than a sentence under a chart.

Two camps

abandon the axis

A tug-bar sized by headcount answers “is there a split” in one glance; the columns answer “who”; the gutter is where the mean goes to die.

What it fixes
  • Side is structural, not chromatic — it survives colour blindness and greyscale printing.
  • Organisation names get their own tile and can never overlap, at any headcount.
  • The unanimous case reads correctly: one camp is empty and says so in words.
  • The mean sitting in the gutter is the “reading nobody holds” claim.
What it costs
  • Absolute position is only readable per-tile; you cannot see at a glance that 80 and 60 are 20 apart.
  • It breaks continuity with the Verdict tab's histogram, which the spec leans on.
  • An uneven split leaves one column much taller than the other.
Proposal 03

Lane ledger

The strip and the table are the same four facts in two objects, so make them one object. One row per organisation: the name in a fixed column that cannot collide, and a shared 0–100 lane where a bar grows from the 50 pivot toward that organisation's score. The empty middle becomes a shaded column crossing every lane, which is the most emphatic way a page can say “nobody”.

Lane ledger

fuse chart and table

Bar direction is the side, bar length is the conviction, and the gap is a column of white space you cannot read past.

What it fixes
  • One object instead of three; every number appears exactly once.
  • Names live in a fixed column, so collision is structurally impossible.
  • Deviation from the 50 pivot makes “Neutral · 45” visibly a hair to the left of centre.
  • Scales to any headcount by growing rows, the direction a card can afford to grow.
What it costs
  • Tallest of the three at four organisations — one row per org, not one marker.
  • Cluster shape is harder to read than on a single rail: you infer it from row order.
  • Bars grow from the pivot, so a bar can cross the empty column even though no opinion sits in it — the caption has to say falls in, not is in.
  • Needs a real minimum width; below ~320px of lane the bars stop being comparable.
Recommendation

Which one to wire

Lane ledger, with the tug-bar borrowed from Two camps.

Proposal 03 is the only one that removes the redundancy rather than rearranging it, and the only one whose collision-freedom is structural instead of computed — which matters most for the state the fixture cannot show you, an instance with twenty organisations holding opinions. Its shaded gap column crossing every lane is also a stronger statement of the card's thesis than either the bracket or the void.

Its one real weakness is that the split's shape — two clusters, this far apart — has to be inferred from row order. Proposal 02's tug-bar fixes that in thirty pixels: one stacked bar sized by headcount, above the lanes, answering “is there a split and how lopsided” before the reader looks at a single lane. That is the combination worth wiring.

Proposal 01 is the safest choice if continuity with the existing spec matters more than the cleanup: it keeps the strip the PRD describes, keeps the Verdict tab's axis, and needs no rewrite of §6 beyond deleting the histogram paragraph. It also keeps a real risk — label dodging is layout logic that has to be correct on data nobody has seen yet.

All three drop the histogram, drop the .vp-opinion bar, state each score once, replace supports / disputes the value with a side chip, and add the staleness marker. Those changes are independent of which layout wins.
