Analyst Profile — candidate C, the stated judgement

The profile reads as a document rather than a form: each signal is a sentence asserting what a piece of evidence is worth, with the numbers editable in place, sections are chapters, and the simulator is an amendment with a before-and-after of its own. The bet is that an org profile has readers who did not write it — a colleague deciding whether to fork it, an auditor asking why a value scored 84 — and that they should be able to read it without knowing the schema. Thresholds

Where the counted evidence turns into a word. The lean asks how one-sided the record is; the bands ask how much of it there is.

2.1 The lean thresholds.lean_supermajority

The record is read as asserting one thing when of the organisations reporting it agree.

A supermajority on either side, with a tolerance — 34 of 100 against misses the mirror of 0.66 by a floating-point hair.

CHAPTER 4

Exclusions

Evidence you have decided not to count, filtered before any signal sees it — so two signals reading the same fact read the same filtered set. Every row an exclusion removes is listed in the assessment as not counted, naming the rule.

4.1 Self-sightings sightings.self

Sightings filed by the reporting organisation within hour of its own report are not counted; later ones are.

An organisation confirming its own fresh report is not corroboration. Later ones are kept, because “we still see this” is real information. Amended: switched on here; the code would leave it off.

4.2 Mirrored feeds feeds.mirrored

Feeds sharing the same count once rather than separately.

MISP does not record what a feed mirrors, so this folds by the key above — which is wrong where one provider runs unrelated feeds. Amended: switched on here; the code would leave it off.

Age is measured from the .

5.2 The curve relevance.decay_speed

Past days of encoding lag the timeline is flagged uncertain.

What this candidate gives up

The direction. A profile is somebody’s stated judgement, and an org profile has readers who did not write it — a colleague deciding whether to fork it, an auditor asking why a value scored 84. So it is a document: chapters, numbered clauses you can cite, marginal notes, schedules for the things that are maps rather than sentences, errata for what the loader refused, and an amendment for the simulator. There is no second form anywhere; the numbers inside the sentences are the form, and Reading is the same document with them turned back into what they say.

The risk it had to survive is that eleven signals as eleven sentences is a lot of prose to scan, and an analyst who wants to change one number should not have to read a paragraph to find it. Four devices answer it, and they are the design rather than decoration: a right-aligned subject gutter, so the eye scans twelve subjects and not twelve sentences; figures as the only monospace thing on a serif page, so the number is what the eye lands on inside a clause; a contents with dot leaders carrying the figure each clause produced, which is a one-click jump to the number you came for; and change bars ruling the four clauses that depart from the shipped default, so “what did this author actually change” is one scan of the left edge and not a read.

What it gives up. Density: twelve clauses and their prose take about three screens where a table would take one, and a reader who already thinks in the ledger is being shown a paragraph they did not need. And a dropped-in signal has no sentence — clause 1.2 is exactly that case, a signal this instance does not implement whose per_partner and cap no version here can label, so its sentence degrades to the bare keys and their figures. Every future custom signal costs a hand-written line, or gets that fallback. That is the price of the bet.
