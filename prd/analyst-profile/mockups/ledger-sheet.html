Candidate A — the ledger sheet

Everything is one table and the numbers are the design. One ruled sheet per board, one line per signal, figures in a monospace column that adds up — by group, then to the foot — because the exact-sum invariant is the whole reason this feature can show its work. Nothing is normalised anywhere in it, so a column that visibly adds up is not a metaphor for the score; it is the score. Every figure below comes from prd/analyst-profile/09a-fixtures/, and every subtotal was computed from those files rather than typed.

What this candidate gives up

The risk §4 names is real: seven sections do not all fit one table. Three of them are maps — relevance.ttl_days, reference.org_trust and reference.warninglist_category — and forcing them into the signals table would have given twelve rows of two populated columns. The answer here is the one a real ledger has always used for a line that is itself a breakdown: a supporting schedule, ruled identically, folio-referenced from the section that owns it, banked two or three across so twelve short rows do not waste 1600px. A map with set-valued entries (enrichment.auto_run) becomes a matrix — types down, modules across — which is still a ruled table and still reads at a glance. Where a schedule holds letters rather than figures, the letter is joined to what it is worth (org_trust × org_trust_scale) so it lands on the same ruler as everything else.

What it costs: density. This board asks a reader to be comfortable with a sheet of figures, and it says almost nothing in prose that it can say in a column — a colleague who has never seen the schema will read the index board correctly and will have to work at the edit board. It also spends its one colour pair entirely on direction (--vp-dir-with / --vp-dir-against), so bands, states and badges are all carried by ruling and small caps instead.
